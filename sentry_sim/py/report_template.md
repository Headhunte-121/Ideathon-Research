# Real-data replay report: Autonomous Sternal Sentry alarm logic

Generated by `py/build_report.py` from the CSVs in `results/` (re-run it after any experiment). Date of runs: 6 Nov 2026.
Simulator: Vivado xsim 2025.2. Python 3.14, wfdb 4.3.1.

## 0. What this is, and what it is not

The Verilog "virtual device" was replayed on **public recordings of real people** to find out two things that a simulation
with made-up signals cannot tell us: **(a)** does the alarm logic false-alarm on real signals, and **(b)** can the chest
sensor chain still see a living heartbeat. **Nothing here measures a cardiac arrest, ischemia, or any patient outcome.**
No public dataset has an arrest recorded with this sensor set. The arrest itself stays *simulated*.

Allowed poster wording: **"Alarm logic simulated; replayed on public recordings (PhysioNet NSRDB, NSTDB, CEBS; PPG-DaLiA)."**
Not allowed: "validated", "clinically tested", any sensitivity/specificity for arrest or ischemia.

**Headline (plain):**
1. The R-peak detector is very good on clean real ECG (99.95 % mean sensitivity, 99.64 % mean positive predictivity, 18 people) and degrades badly in heavy real noise (57-62 % sensitivity and 45-48 % PPV at -6 dB).
2. The chest-vibration "is the heart still beating" test has a large margin on living, resting people: no second of 20 people x 5 min ever fell below the "still" threshold.
3. The drift detector **raised almost no false check-ins (0.06 per 24 h at K=3.0, none at 3.8 or 4.5), but only because it is nearly blind**: a +15 % heart-rate step was caught in 0 of 18 people. This is a design problem, diagnosed in section 3.4, not a pass.

4. In 36 person-hours of living people in daily life (PPG-DaLiA), wrist pulse loss with a still chest lasted 3 s or more 13 times (2 of 15 people, 11 of them person 8 at lunch). Whether the chest heartbeat-vibration check would have stopped them is untested outside lying-down (section 3.6).

## 3. Results

### 3.1 E1: R-peak detector under real motion/muscle noise (NSTDB) *(Replayed on public recordings, 2 records)*

Method: `py/real_batch.py ecg --noisy`; the ECG is resampled to 250 Hz, scaled to 16 codes/uV, and fed to `rtl/ecg_beat_detector.sv`. A detection within 150 ms of the database beat annotation counts as correct. Noisy minutes are scored separately from the clean minutes of the same record. Both are 30-min, two-lead recordings from 2 people.

<<E1>>

![E1](fig_e1_beat_detector_vs_snr.png)

What it shows: with real noise at 18 dB or better the detector is essentially perfect; at 12 dB it still keeps 98-99 % of beats but already adds 27-45 false detections in the noisy minutes; at 0 dB about 1 beat in 5 is missed and **about 2 detections in 5 are wrong** (PPV 57-61 %); at -6 dB sensitivity is 57-62 % and **more than half of all detections are wrong** (PPV 45-48 %).
Diagnosis: positive predictivity is lower than sensitivity at every noisy level, so the main failure is the detector *firing on noise bursts* (its threshold follows the loudest recent slope, and electrode-motion noise has slopes comparable to a QRS), not only missing beats. *Update after re-testing:* this section first suggested that my ADC emulation (16 codes/uV, +/-2.05 mV of range, 7-19 % of samples at the rail at 0 dB and below) was a second cause. **That was tested (results/TWEAKS_REPORT.md, tweak 3) and is not supported**: at 4 codes/uV only 1.5 % of samples clip, yet sensitivity at 0 dB fell from 80 % to 71 % and at -6 dB from 60 % to 55 %. The loss in heavy noise is the detector's, not a scaling artefact.
What it does NOT show: nothing about arrhythmia or arrest; two records only.

### 3.3 E3: drift detector false check-ins on about 20 h of real heart rate per person (NSRDB) *(Replayed on public recordings, 18 people, 382 person-hours)*

Method: heart rate per beat (from the database's own beat annotations, not from my detector) fed to `rtl/baseline_drift_detector.sv` as configured in `tb/tb_drift_false_alarms.sv`: learn on the first 8192 beats (about 2 h), then flag after 5 consecutive 60-beat windows with at least 30 beats beyond K sigma, baseline allowed to adapt slowly (alpha = 2^-10). K = 3.0, 3.8, 4.5. Hours are real elapsed time (sum of RR intervals); the testbench's own printed "per 24 h" uses a slightly different estimate, so its figures can differ in the second decimal.

<<E3A>>

![E3a](fig_e3_false_checkins_per_person.png)

**Result, as built: 1 false check-in in 382 person-hours at K=3.0 (person 16272); 0 at K=3.8 and 4.5. Target (at most 1 per person per 24 h) met for everybody.**

**But the detector also barely detects anything.** For each person a step change in heart rate was added over the last quarter of the recording (about 5 h) and the delay to the first flag measured by the Verilog:

![E3b](fig_e3_detection_vs_shift.png)

*Replayed on public recordings + Simulated injection.* A +15 % rise (for example 70 to 80 bpm), a sustained 5 h shift, is flagged for **nobody** at any K. +25 % is flagged for 2 of 18 people at K=3.0, after 2.5-3 h, and for nobody at 3.8 or 4.5. The "zero false check-ins" result must therefore not be read as a pass: with this input the detector is mostly silent.

### 3.4 E3 diagnosis and proposal (nothing was tuned; no RTL was changed)

Tool: `py/drift_model.py`, a bit-exact Python copy of the RTL detector. It reproduces the Verilog's false-flag counts on every record checked and its first-flag beat to within 1 beat (13 603 vs 13 604 on person 16420 at +25 %, K=3.0). *Calculated.*

Cause 1, **the spread of real heart rate is far larger than the synthetic stand-in.** Learned over the first 2 h, the standard deviation of per-beat heart rate is 7.0-15.7 bpm (mean 10.9 bpm); the made-up test used 2.5 bpm. A +15 % step is only 0.7-2.0 sigma (mean 1.2 sigma) and +25 % is 1.2-3.4 sigma (mean 2.0 sigma), always below a 3.8 sigma threshold.
Cause 2, **it is not beat-to-beat noise; it is slow variation (activity, time of day).** Averaging 120 beats first leaves the spread almost unchanged (5-14 bpm), so +25 % is still only 2.5 sigma at the median. The table below feeds the same detector a causal N-beat average (Python only, same settings):

<<E3C>>

Smoothing helps a little but not enough, and false check-ins rise at K=3.0 (up to 2.1 per 24 h for one person). *Calculated on public recordings (NSRDB), not RTL.*
**Proposal (untested here):** the design intent is *resting* heart rate. The detector should only see beats from moments when the Pod says the person is still (low chest motion), so that walking, stairs and meals do not widen the learned spread. NSRDB has no motion channel, so this cannot be tested on it; PPG-DaLiA has activity labels and a chest accelerometer and could test the principle on about 2 h per person (<<E5NOTE>>). Until then: **do not claim sensitivity to heart-rate drift from these numbers.** The honest statement is "false check-ins on real all-day heart rate: about 0 (nearly blind as built)".
Other limits: NSRDB people are healthy; the detector learned from the first 2 h of a recording that starts at an arbitrary time of day, so circadian changes are part of what it sees.

### 3.5 E4: does a living person's chest vibration stay above the "chest still" threshold? (CEBS) *(Replayed on public recordings, 20 people x 2 x 5 min)*

Method: `py/real_scg_cebs.py`. The CEBS accelerometer channel (LIS344ALH, 5 kHz, analog band 0.5-100 Hz) is resampled to 250 Hz, high-passed at 1 Hz, converted to g, expressed in LSM6DSOX codes (122 ug/LSB), and averaged as |a| per second, which is what the Pod's "chest vibration" summary does. A second counts as "still" if this is below `SCG_THR` = 5 codes (about 0.6 mg).

**Sensitivity number (the one input that cannot be read from the files).** The record header gives the channel gain as 2462.6 ADC units per mV (mV at the Biopac input), and the dataset paper (Garcia-Gonzalez et al., CinC 2013) states the sensor model and band but **no mV/g and no conditioning-circuit gain**. The mV/g value was taken from the **LIS344ALH datasheet** (*Cited, from search excerpts of the datasheet's mechanical table; the PDF itself could not be opened*): sensitivity = Vdd/5 in the +/-2 g range = **660 mV/g at 3.3 V**, and Vdd/15 = 220 mV/g in the +/-6 g range. **Which range pin setting and supply the CEBS authors used, and whether any amplifier sat between sensor and Biopac, is not documented**, so both values are reported. Everything scales by their ratio of 3. No value was guessed beyond these two datasheet figures. As a plausibility check (not proof): at 660 mV/g the signal's RMS is 2.2 mV, i.e. 3.4 mg, a normal order of magnitude for chest SCG.

<<E4>>

![E4](fig_e4_chest_vibration_vs_still_threshold.png)

What it shows: at either sensitivity, **0 % of the seconds of any of the 20 resting people fall below 5 codes** (nor below 8). At 660 mV/g the weakest person's median is 12.4 codes, 2.5 times the threshold; at 220 mV/g it is 37 codes. A threshold of 12 would have called 40.7 % of person b003's living seconds "still", so **12 is too high; 5 is not**. Where the energy comes from (`results/e4_band_energy_baseline_660mVg.csv`): the 5-25 Hz heartbeat band carries a median 16.7 codes, breathing/sway (1-5 Hz) 6.9, and the 40-100 Hz band, a proxy for the sensor's noise floor, 1.75; the heartbeat band is 11 times the noise floor at the median (range 5-51).
The real signal is much weaker than the model's: model/body_physiology_model.sv gives a heartbeat peak of 20 mg (0.25 x 80 000 ug); here the mean |a| of a person is 1.5-6 mg (12-50 codes at 660 mV/g). The threshold of 5 codes still sits below every living second, but with a 2.5x margin for the weakest person, not the model's large one.
**What it does NOT show:** (1) what the sensor reads when there is **no** heartbeat. That is the other half of the arrest test and needs the Pod IMU's own noise floor; the CEBS high-frequency band (1.75 codes, below 5) is the only hint, for a different sensor. (2) People who are standing, sitting, breathing hard, obese, or wearing clothing between sensor and chest; CEBS people lay supine on a bed. (3) The LSM6DSOX's gravity (about 1 g DC): the CEBS channel is AC-coupled (0.5 Hz), so the high-pass the Pod would need was not exercised.

## 4. Failures found and fixed while running on real files

See `results/CHANGELOG_real_data.md`. In short: no ECG had any detections until the DC offset of real recordings (NSTDB sits near -6 mV) was removed, because it clipped every sample to the 16-bit rail; xsim plus-arguments were being split at `=`; the downloader script named in the brief did not exist; CEBS record names in the README were wrong (`a001` does not exist). None changed the RTL.

## 5. Things the numbers do not support (read before using any of this on the poster)

*This report is the original; follow-up changes tried afterwards are in `results/TWEAKS_REPORT.md`.*

- Nothing about detecting cardiac arrest or ischemia, or any outcome.
- The drift detector's low false-alarm count is a consequence of low sensitivity (section 3.3), not evidence of a good detector.
- "Chest still" has only been tested from the living side.
- All people are healthy volunteers/ambulatory adults from three small databases; none wore the intended sensors.
